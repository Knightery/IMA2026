# Human Judgment Required for a Winning Story

## Core Conclusion

The case is not really asking for nine disconnected answers. It is asking for one persuasive management story supported by nine analyses.

The programmatic work can find patterns, rank cities, and calculate scenario outcomes. The human work has to decide what those patterns mean, why they matter, and why West Valley should act on them now.

## Which Questions Require Human Thinking

- `Q1`: Low creativity. Mostly factual. Human value is in the visualization and the headline, not the answer itself.
- `Q2`: Low creativity. Same dynamic as Q1. The insight is quantitative; the human job is to make the contrast between scale and organic readiness obvious.
- `Q3`: Medium creativity. The regression is mechanical, but the interpretation is not. A human has to stop the deck from making a lazy "price drives demand" claim.
- `Q4`: Very high creativity. This is the first explicitly inferential question. The current answer risks sounding generic unless a human explains why Los Angeles wins scale, why Seattle wins penetration, and why the Northwest supports premium pricing.
- `Q5`: Medium-high creativity. The city ranking can be automated. The recommendation logic cannot. A human must explain why a 5-city western cluster is strategically smarter than simply choosing the five highest prices.
- `Q6`: Medium creativity. The scenario math is mechanical; the business response is not. Human judgment is needed to explain what freight volatility means operationally.
- `Q7`: High creativity. "The same five win" is not enough. A human has to turn stability into a strategic insight: resilience, margin compression, and what management should do about it.
- `Q8`: Medium-high creativity. Seasonality charts can be generated. The meaningful pattern selection and the framing of the "duck curve" are human.
- `Q9`: Very high creativity. This is managerial design, not just analysis. Contracting, trade strategy, promotions, retailer relationships, and S&OP are not things the data can invent by itself.

## The Story the Deck Should Tell

The winning story is not:

"Here are the answers to nine homework questions."

It is:

"West Valley can enter organic Hass avocados profitably, but only if it treats this as a disciplined learning pilot rather than a broad market launch."

That story should move in this order:

1. The market is attractive, but uneven.
   Los Angeles proves the category is big. Seattle and similar markets prove premium organic demand is real. The point is not only where avocados are sold, but where West Valley can win first.
2. The biggest market is not automatically the best launch market.
   Los Angeles dominates sales, yet it is not the best pilot city. That contrast is one of the strongest strategic moments in the case and should be highlighted, not buried.
3. The best entry is a coherent western cluster, not five random high-profit dots.
   The recommendation is stronger when framed as a lane system and operating model, not just a sorted profit table.
4. The recommendation is resilient, but not effortless.
   Freight shocks do not change the top five, but they do change margin. That means the strategy is robust while the operating discipline still matters.
5. Seasonality is not a side analysis; it is the playbook.
   The case wants more than market ranking. It wants evidence that West Valley can actually run the business through high-volume and high-price periods.
6. The real win is a pilot that buys learning at positive margin.
   West Valley is not trying to win the whole U.S. avocado market in 2026. It is buying a low-regret growth option.

## Where Human Creativity Must Be Added

- `Q4 city logic`
  The deck needs real city-specific reasoning, not placeholders like "scale" or "consumer mix."
  Human task: write convincing, careful inference for Los Angeles, Seattle, Portland, Boise, Spokane, and San Diego.
  Important caveat: if you use claims like demographics, food culture, or health orientation, either source them or label them as informed inference so they do not read like hallucinations.

- `The LA paradox`
  This is one of the strongest story moments available.
  Human task: explicitly say, "Los Angeles proves demand exists, but it is not the best test market for a premium pilot."
  That is a strategic insight, not a spreadsheet output.

- `The cluster thesis`
  The recommendation should feel designed.
  Human task: make clear that this is not five isolated bets. It is one western pilot system with shared lanes, similar consumer logic, and manageable oversight.

- `Buyer-side creativity`
  The case is B2B. West Valley sells to retailers and restaurants.
  Human task: sharpen the story around retailer sell-in, food-service usage, seasonal promotions, and why a buyer would take shelf or menu risk on a new supplier.

- `Competitive response`
  The case explicitly says the industry is highly competitive.
  Human task: insert a believable competitor reaction story.
  Example: incumbents may discount, bundle, or defend accounts. West Valley's answer is not brute-force expansion; it is a measured pilot with account selection and service reliability.

- `Q7 interpretation`
  "The ranking does not change" is analytically correct but narratively weak.
  Human task: translate this into management language.
  Example: "Freight volatility does not overturn the strategy, but it does narrow the margin for execution error."

- `Q9 operating imagination`
  This is where the deck can feel like a real executive recommendation instead of a student analysis.
  Human task: make the seasonality response tangible.
  Not just "use S&OP," but who meets, what decision changes monthly, what promotions run in soft months, and how growers and retailers share the plan.

- `Opening and close`
  These cannot be generic.
  Human task: write the opening and final recommendation in a voice that feels decisive.
  Good finalist-style lines include:
  - "Do not enter broadly. Win a western pilot first."
  - "Price support chooses the cities; operating discipline makes them profitable."
  - "The goal of 2026 is not scale. It is proof."

## Highest-Priority Human Pass

If time is limited, concentrate human effort on:

- slides 29-32 / seasonality operating playbook
- slides 36-39 / competitor response, risk, and final close

These are the places where creativity changes the deck from correct to convincing.
